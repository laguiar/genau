## 2. The rule in words

**When you write Präteritum at A2/B1.** In an e-mail or letter, keep to the Perfekt — except for *sein, haben, werden*, the modals, *wissen* and *es gab*, which are always Präteritum: *Gestern **war** ich krank und **konnte** nicht kommen. Ich **hatte** Fieber.* Mixing *Ich war … und habe …* in the same paragraph is exactly right.

**When you read Präteritum.** Newspaper articles, DW news, stories, biographies and the exam's longer reading texts are written in the Präteritum. You do not need to produce *ging, nahm, sah*; you need to **recognise** them instantly and map them to the infinitive. The list above covers most of what you will meet; the full table is in [shared/verbs.md §5](../shared/verbs.md), organised by vowel family so the patterns help (*i-a-u: trinken – trank; ei-ie-ie: bleiben – blieb; e-a: geben – gab, sehen – sah, sprechen – sprach*).

**Forms to produce.** Regular: *machte, lernte, wohnte, arbeitete, öffnete*. Modals and *haben*: *hatte, konnte, musste, wollte, durfte, sollte, mochte* (A1-17). *werden → wurde, wurdest, wurde, wurden, wurdet, wurden*. *wissen → wusste*. *sein → war*. Endings for strong verbs: none in *ich/er*, *-st, -en, -t, -en*: *ich ging, du gingst, er ging, wir gingen, ihr gingt, sie gingen*.

**Präteritum with `als`** (single event in the past) and in *Als ich Kind war, …* is the standard combination you will need in B1 writing (B1-04).

> 🇧🇷 **Portuguese contrast** — PT has one simple past for speech and writing (*fui, comi*); German splits: spoken/letters = Perfekt, narrative prose = Präteritum. Reading a German news text in the Präteritum feels like the PT *pretérito perfeito*; do not assume the imperfeito nuance. Many strong-verb vowel changes look like PT irregulars (*fazer – fiz; ter – tive*) — same kind of memorisation.
> 🇪🇸 **Spanish contrast** — When you read German news articles or stories (Leseverstehen!), you will see the **Präteritum** (*er ging, sie kam, wir sahen, er dachte*). Do not try to map Präteritum to Spanish *indefinido* (*fue*) and Perfekt to *pretérito perfecto* (*ha ido*) — in German they have the **exact same meaning** and only differ by text type (Präteritum in written narratives and for `war/hatte/konnte/wusste/es gab`; Perfekt in speech and letters).
> 🇫🇷 **French contrast** — Just as French reserves *passé simple* (*il alla, ils virent*) for written literature/history, German uses the **Präteritum** of full verbs (*er ging, sie sahen, er kam*) mainly in written narrative (newspaper articles, stories — key for Lesen!). However, for `sein, haben, werden`, the modals, `wissen` and `es gab`, German uses Präteritum **everywhere** (speech, e-mails, letters), where French would use *imparfait* or *passé composé*.
> 🇬🇧 **English contrast** — The Präteritum is the *I went / I saw* form and many strong verbs rhyme with English (*kam – came, sah – saw, trank – drank, sang – sang, fand – found, begann – began*). Use these as hooks.

## 3. Watch out

- ~~ich gehte, ich sehte~~ → *ging, sah.* Strong verbs never take *-te*.
- ~~er kamt~~ → *er kam*; ~~ihr kamen~~ → *ihr kamt*.
- Present *ich weiß* / past *ich wusste* / Perfekt *habe gewusst*.
- Regular verbs with stem in *-t/-d*: *arbeit**ete**, wart**ete**, red**ete***.
- *wurde* (became / was, past of *werden*) vs *würde* (would, Konjunktiv II, A2-13).
