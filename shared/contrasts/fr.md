# Language Pack — French (`fr`)

**Code:** `fr` · **Language:** French · **Flag:** 🇫🇷 · **Label:** French contrast

Master reference for French speakers (or learners who know French as an L2/L3) learning German up to telc B1. `python3 review/review.py lang sync` uses the `## Topic notes` section below to populate the `> 🇫🇷 **French contrast**` boxes across `A1/`, `A2/`, and `B1/`.

## 1. Nouns and articles

| German feature | French transfer | Contrast note & key traps |
|---|---|---|
| Three genders (`der / die / das`) | helps (2 arbitrary genders: *le / la*), but gender does not transfer | Learn every noun with `der/die/das`: *le soleil* → **die** Sonne, *la lune* → **der** Mond, *la voiture* → **das** Auto. Notice `-age` is mostly masculine in FR (*le garage*) but **feminine (`die`)** in German (*die Garage, die Etage*)! |
| Article agreement | helps (*le/la/les, un/une*) | German articles also inflect for **four cases** (`● ▲ ■ ◆`), not only gender and number. |
| Noun capitalisation | misleads | Every German noun is capitalised (*das Haus, das Lesen*). Adjectives of nationality are lowercase (*französisch*), unlike nouns (*Französisch, ein Franzose*). |
| Plural: many patterns + umlaut | neither (FR adds silent `-s/-x`) | German plural endings are pronounced (`-e, -er, -(e)n, -s`, `¨`). Learn the plural with the noun. |
| Compound nouns | misleads (FR uses `N + de/à + N`: *salle à manger*) | German fuses compounds from right to left; the **last** word sets the gender (*das Esszimmer* ← *das Zimmer*). |
| Zero article & professions | helps! (*Il est médecin*) | Like French (*Il est professeur*), German uses no article after `sein/werden` for professions and nationalities (*Er ist Lehrer*). Also no partitive article (*du pain / de l'eau* → bare *Brot / Wasser*!). |

## 2. Cases (`● ▲ ■ ◆`)

| German feature | French transfer | Contrast note & key traps |
|---|---|---|
| Four cases on determiners/adjectives/pronouns | helps via pronouns (*il / le / lui*) | Anchor cases to French clitic pronouns: `●` = *il/elle*, `▲` = *le/la* (COD), `■` = *lui/leur* (COI), `◆` = *de / dont*. |
| `■` Dativ for COI & `■` verbs | helps for COI (*plaire à → gefallen, appartenir à → gehören, répondre à → antworten*), misleads for *aider / remercier / suivre* | *plaire à, appartenir à, ressembler à, répondre à, nuire à, faire mal à* are COI in French and `■` in German! Watch the exceptions that are COD in French but `■` in German: *aider qn → **helfen ■**, remercier qn → **danken ■**, suivre qn → **folgen ■**, féliciter qn → **gratulieren ■***. |
| Two-way prepositions (`wo? ■` vs `wohin? ▲`) | misleads (FR *dans / sur* does not distinguish location vs destination) | *Je suis dans la ville* → *in **der** Stadt (`■`)* vs *Je vais dans la ville* → *in **die** Stadt (`▲`)*. |
| `◆` Genitiv = possession | helps (*la voiture **du** directeur*) | Written German uses `◆` (*das Auto **des** Direktors*); spoken German uses `von + ■`. |

## 3. Verbs and tenses

| German feature | French transfer | Contrast note & key traps |
|---|---|---|
| Obligatory subject pronoun | helps! (*je, tu, il…*) | Unlike Spanish/Portuguese, French and German both require the subject pronoun (*Je viens → Ich komme*), and both use dummy *il / es* (*Il pleut → Es regnet*). |
| One verb `sein` | helps! (*être*) | Like French *être*, German `sein` covers both permanent and temporary states (*Je suis fatigué / professeur*). Watch *avoir faim/soif/peur/raison* → *Hunger/Durst/Angst/Recht **haben*** (same!), except age: *J'ai 30 ans* → *Ich **bin** 30 Jahre alt*! |
| `du / ihr / Sie` | helps (*tu / vous*), with one split | French *vous* covers both polite singular/plural AND informal plural. German splits them: informal plural ("you guys") = **ihr**; polite singular/plural = **Sie** (3rd person plural verb). |
| Perfekt with `haben` AND `sein` | **strongest anchor!** (*avoir* vs *être* in *passé composé*) | French *passé composé* (*j'ai mangé / je suis allé(e)*) is the exact structural twin of German Perfekt (*ich habe gegessen / ich bin gegangen*)! Even the `sein` list (movement A→B, change of state, *bleiben/rester*, *werden/devenir*) matches French *être* verbs — **except** reflexive verbs (*je me suis lavé* → *ich **habe** mich gewaschen*: always `haben`!) and *sein* itself (*j'ai été* → *ich **bin** gewesen*). |
| Präteritum vs Perfekt | helps! (*passé simple* vs *passé composé*) | Just as French uses *passé composé* in speech/letters and *passé simple / imparfait* in written narrative, German uses **Perfekt** in speech/letters and **Präteritum** in narrative texts (plus `war, hatte, konnte…` everywhere). |
| `seit` + Präsens | **exact match!** (*depuis* + présent) | *J'habite ici **depuis** trois ans* = *Ich wohne **seit** drei Jahren hier* (both use present tense!). |
| Modal verbs | helps (*pouvoir, devoir, vouloir*) | *ne pas devoir* in FR often means prohibition; in German, *nicht müssen* = don't have to (*ne pas être obligé de*), while prohibition = **nicht dürfen**. |
| Konjunktiv II (`würde, hätte, wäre`) | helps (*conditionnel* `-rais` & *si j'avais*) | French *Si j'avais (imparfait) le temps, je viendrais (conditionnel)* uses **Konjunktiv II in BOTH clauses** in German: *Wenn ich Zeit **hätte**, **würde** ich kommen*. |
| Passiv (`werden` vs `sein`) | misleads (*être* + participe passé for both) | French *La porte est ouverte (à 8h / maintenant)* uses *être* for both; German separates **wird geöffnet** (action) vs **ist geöffnet** (state). French *on* = German **man**! |

## 4. Word order and connectors

| German feature | French transfer | Contrast note & key traps |
|---|---|---|
| V2 inversion (*Morgen fahre ich*) | misleads (*Demain, je pars*) | Fronting any time, place, object, or subordinate clause pushes the subject **behind** the conjugated verb: *Demain je travaille* → *Morgen **arbeite ich***. |
| Verb-final in Nebensatz (`weil, dass, wenn, ob`) | misleads (*parce que je suis…*) | Every Subjunktor sends the conjugated verb to the **very end**: *…, weil ich krank **bin***. |
| Negation (`nicht / kein / nie / nichts / niemand`) | helps & misleads (*ne … pas / plus / jamais / rien / personne*) | German has no *ne*; single negative word per clause (*Je n'ai **rien** vu → Ich habe **nichts** gesehen*). French *pas de + noun* (*Je n'ai **pas de** voiture / **plus de** temps*) = German **kein** (*kein Auto / **keine** Zeit **mehr***)! |
| Pronoun order (`es ihm`) | **helps!** (*le lui*) | In standard French (*Je **le lui** donne*), 3rd-person COD (*le/la/les*) comes **before** COI (*lui/leur*) — the exact same order as German (`▲` pronoun before `■` pronoun: *Ich gebe **es ihm***)! |
| `um … zu / ohne … zu / statt … zu` | helps (*pour / sans / au lieu de + infinitif*) | Same same-subject rule as French! Just split the two parts around the objects: *pour acheter un billet* → ***um** ein Ticket **zu kaufen***. |

## 5. Spelling, sounds, and false friends

- **Sounds & spelling:** German `h` is aspirated (*Haus*); `ch` after *a/o/u* is guttural, after *e/i/ä/ö/ü* palatal; `u` = FR *ou* (*gut*), while `ü` = FR *u* (*Tür* = *tu*); `ö` = FR *eu* (*schön*); `v` = "f" (*Vater*), `w` = "v" (*Wasser*); `z` = "ts" (*Zeit*). · **French loanwords in German:** Watch gender shifts! Words in **`-age`** are feminine in German (*die Garage, die Etage, die Bagage*), whereas *das Büro, das Hotel, das Restaurant, das Ticket, das Niveau, das Abonnement* are neuter. Verbs in **`-ieren`** (*studieren, reservieren, reparieren, telefonieren, funktionieren*) come from French *-er/-ir* and **never take `ge-`** in Partizip II! · **False friends FR ↔ DE:** *aktuell* = current (FR *actuel*, not English!), *eventuell* = possibly (FR *éventuellement*), *der Chef* = boss (not cook = *der Koch*), *das Kuvert / der Umschlag* = envelope, *sympathisch* = likeable (FR *sympathique*), *brav* = well-behaved (not brave = *mutig*), *fast* = almost (*presque*).

## Topic notes

### A1-01
French never capitalises common nouns, so train your eye to capitalise every noun (*das Haus, der Montag*). German `u` sounds like French *ou* (*gut*), while `ü` sounds like French *u* (*Tür*); `ö` sounds like French *eu* (*schön*). German has no acute/grave accents (*é, è, à*) or cedilla (*ç*) — only `ä, ö, ü` and `ß`. In letters, the salutation ends with a comma and the next line starts **lowercase** (unlike French *Madame, / Je vous écris…*).

### A1-02
Like French (*je, tu, il*), German **never drops the subject pronoun** (*Ich wohne in Berlin*), and has no continuous tense (*Je suis en train de travailler* → *Ich arbeite gerade*). However, French *vous* splits into **two** pronouns in German: **ihr** for two or more friends/family members (*ihr wohnt*), and **Sie** (capitalised, verb in `-en`) for formal singular or plural (*Sie wohnen*).

### A1-03
German `sein` matches French *être*, and many French *avoir* expressions match German `haben` (*avoir faim/soif/peur/raison/chaud* → *Hunger/Durst/Angst/Recht **haben***, though *j'ai chaud/froid* = *mir ist warm/kalt*). Major trap: age uses **sein**, never *haben* (*J'ai 30 ans* → *Ich **bin** 30 Jahre alt*). Stem vowel changes in strong verbs (*du sprichst, er liest, er fährt*) only affect `du` and `er/sie/es`, similar to irregular singular stems in French (*je bois / nous buvons*).

### A1-04
In French, you can put an adverb in front and keep `Subject + Verb` (*Demain, je travaille*). In German, the conjugated verb is locked in **Position 2**, so the subject must move behind the verb: *Morgen **arbeite ich***. For yes/no questions, simply put the verb first (*Habites-tu à Berlin ?* → ***Wohnst** du in Berlin?* — no *est-ce que*!).

### A1-05
Like French (*le / la*), German grammatical gender is arbitrary and must be learned with the noun — do not trust the French gender (*le soleil → **die** Sonne, la lune → **der** Mond, la table → **der** Tisch*). Watch French loanwords ending in **`-age`**: they are masculine in French (*le garage, l'étage*) but **feminine (`die`)** in German (*die Garage, die Etage*). Like French (*Il est médecin*), omit the article before professions and nationalities after *sein* (*Er ist Arzt*).

### A1-06
In French, the plural `-s` or `-x` is mostly silent and only heard on the article (*les enfants*). In German, every plural noun changes its spoken ending (`-e, -er, -(e)n, -s`) and often adds an umlaut (*das Haus → die Häuser*). The `-s` plural (*die Autos, die Büros, die Hotels*) is used mainly for foreign loanwords, not native German nouns.

### A1-07
French two-part negation correlates neatly with German: **ne … pas + verb/adjective** = **nicht** (*Je ne viens pas → Ich komme **nicht***), while **ne … pas de + noun** = **kein-** (*Je n'ai **pas de** voiture / **pas le** temps → Ich habe **kein** Auto / **keine** Zeit*)! And to answer a negative question affirmatively (*Tu ne viens pas ? — **Si !***), German has the exact equivalent of French **Si !**: **Doch!**

### A1-08
Think of `● Nominativ` as the French subject (*il / qui*) and `▲ Akkusativ` as the French COD (*le / que*). Only the masculine singular article changes in `▲` (*der → **den**, ein → **einen**, kein → **keinen***). French has no partitive article in German: *Je bois **du** café / je cherche **des** amis* → *Ich trinke **—** Kaffee / ich suche **—** Freunde* (zero article!).

### A1-09
In French, *son / sa / ses* depends only on the gender of the possessed noun (*son frère, sa sœur* = both "his" and "her"). German works in two steps: first pick the **stem by the owner** (**sein-** = his/its, **ihr-** = her/their), then add the **ending by the noun's gender and case** (*sein Bruder, seine Schwester* vs *ihr Bruder, ihre Schwester*).

### A1-10
French has no separable verbs (*appeler, se lever, commencer* stay in one piece). In a German main clause, the stressed prefix splits off and travels to the **very end** of the clause (*J'appelle mon frère demain* → *Ich **rufe** morgen meinen Bruder **an***), forming the *Satzklammer* (sentence bracket).

### A1-11
French *pouvoir, devoir, vouloir + infinitif* matches German modal verbs, except the main infinitive goes to the **very end** of the clause (*Je dois travailler aujourd'hui* → *Ich **muss** heute **arbeiten***). For polite requests, French *je voudrais* = German **ich möchte**. Unlike French *J'aime nager*, `mögen` cannot take an infinitive — use **verb + gern** (*Ich schwimme gern*).

### A1-12
Like French (*Viens ! Venez !*), the German imperative puts the verb first and drops the informal pronouns `du` and `ihr` (*Komm! Kommt!*), while the formal imperative keeps `Sie` right after the verb (*Kommen Sie bitte!*). Just as French splits *tu* and *vous*, remember to use `ihr` (*Kommt!*) when addressing two or more friends.

### A1-13
German numbers from 21 to 99 put the units before the tens (*vierundzwanzig* = "four-and-twenty", like French *vingt et un* flipped). Watch **`halb`**: French *huit heures et demie* (8:30) looks back at 8, whereas German **halb neun** (8:30 = "half towards nine") looks forward to the next hour! Time prepositions: *à 8 heures → **um** 8 Uhr*; *lundi / le lundi → **am** Montag*; *en mai / en été → **im** Mai / **im** Sommer* (no preposition before a standalone year: *en 2026 → **2026***).

### A1-14
French uses *à* or *en* for both being in a place and going to a place (*Je suis à Berlin / Je vais à Berlin*). German separates location (**in** Berlin), destination without article (**nach** Berlin / **nach** Frankreich / **nach** Hause), and destination to a person or business (**zu** Paul / **zum** Arzt = French *chez le médecin*!). Country with article: *en Suisse → **in der** Schweiz (wo?) / **in die** Schweiz (wohin?)*.

### A1-15
The German `■ Dativ` corresponds closely to the French COI (*complément d'objet indirect* with *à → lui / leur*). Many German `■` verbs are also COI in French: *plaire à → **gefallen**, appartenir à → **gehören**, répondre à → **antworten**, manquer à → **fehlen**, faire mal à → **wehtun***. Watch the crucial exceptions that take a direct object (COD) in French but **`■` Dativ** in German: *aider qn → **helfen + ■*** (*Ich helfe **dir***), *remercier qn → **danken + ■*** (*Ich danke **Ihnen***), *suivre qn → **folgen + ■***.

### A1-16
**Your best French superpower:** German Perfekt works just like French *passé composé*! Both use two auxiliaries: **haben** (*avoir*) for most verbs and all transitive verbs, and **sein** (*être*) for movement from A to B (*gehen/aller, kommen/venir, fahren/partir, fallen/tomber*), change of state (*sterben/mourir, werden/devenir, geboren werden/naître*), and *bleiben* (*rester*)! Two differences to remember: (1) the Partizip II goes to the **very end** of the clause, and (2) French-origin verbs in **`-ieren`** (*studieren, telefonieren, reparieren*) never take `ge-` (*studiert, telefoniert*).

### A1-17
In French, you distinguish *imparfait* (*j'étais, j'avais, je pouvais*) from *passé composé* (*j'ai été, j'ai eu, j'ai pu*) by aspect. In German, for `sein`, `haben`, `werden`, and the modal verbs, you simply use the **Präteritum** (*ich war, hatte, konnte, musste, wollte*) for **every** past context in speech and letters alike. And French *il y avait* = **es gab**.

### A1-18
Unlike French (*une grande maison* vs *la maison est grande*, where *grande* keeps its feminine `-e` in both positions), a German adjective after `sein / werden / bleiben` (**predicative**) never takes an ending (*Das Haus ist **groß**; die Häuser sind **groß***). Adjectives also double as adverbs without adding *-ment* (*schnell* = *rapide* AND *rapidement*). French *aimer + infinitif* (*J'aime cuisiner*) = **verb + gern** (*Ich koche gern*); *préférer* = **lieber**.

### A1-19
French *et, ou, mais, car* match German position-0 connectors (**und, oder, aber, denn** — no inversion). However, linking adverbs like *puis / ensuite / d'abord* (*dann, danach, zuerst*) occupy **Position 1** and force subject-verb inversion (*Puis je rentre → Dann **gehe ich** nach Hause*). Inside the clause, German puts **Time before Place** (*Je vais à Berlin demain → Ich fahre **morgen nach Berlin***).

### A1-20
French **on** (*On parle allemand ici*) maps directly to German **man** (*Hier spricht **man** Deutsch* — 3rd person singular!). For inanimate nouns, look up the German grammatical gender before picking the pronoun: *la table (f) → der Tisch (m) → **er***; *le livre (m) → das Buch (n) → **es***. And French *quelque chose de nouveau / rien de spécial* needs no *de* in German: **etwas Neues / nichts Besonderes**.

### A2-01
Like French COI (*Je donne le livre **à l'enfant***), German uses `■ Dativ` for the recipient and `▲ Akkusativ` for the thing (*Ich gebe **dem Kind** das Buch*). Look at the order when **both** objects are 3rd-person pronouns: standard French says *Je **le lui** donne* (COD *le/la/les* before COI *lui/leur*) — German uses the **exact same order**: `▲` pronoun before `■` pronoun (*Ich gebe **es ihm** / **ihn ihr***)! Also remember that German puts `es/ihn/sie` before `mir/dir/uns/euch` too (*Il me le donne → Er gibt **es mir***).

### A2-02
French uses the same preposition (*dans, sur, sous, devant, derrière*) whether something is stationary or moving there (*Je pose le livre **sur la** table / Le livre est **sur la** table*). German uses one set of nine prepositions (`an, auf, hinter, in, neben, über, unter, vor, zwischen`) and switches the **case**: **wo?** (location, no change of place) → **`■` Dativ** (*auf **dem** Tisch*); **wohin?** (destination/movement into) → **`▲` Akkusativ** (*auf **den** Tisch*).

### A2-03
Memorise the fixed-case groups as two rhythmic chants: `▲` *durch-für-gegen-ohne-um* and `■` *aus-bei-mit-nach-seit-von-zu*. French *chez* (at someone's place/doctor's) = **bei + ■** (*chez le médecin → **beim** Arzt*); *aller chez* = **zu + ■** (*aller chez le médecin → **zum** Arzt*). And just like French *depuis + présent* (*J'habite ici depuis 3 ans*), German **seit + ■** takes the **Präsens** (*Ich wohne seit 3 Jahren hier*).

### A2-04
French and German align almost 1:1 on time prepositions! *depuis 3 ans* (still ongoing, present tense!) = **seit + ■** (*seit drei Jahren*, Präsens!) · *il y a 3 ans* (ago, past tense) = **vor + ■** (*vor drei Jahren*, Perfekt/Präteritum) · *dans une heure* (in an hour from now) = **in + ■** (*in einer Stunde*) · *pendant le cours* = **während + ◆** (*während des Kurses*) · *ne … que* (time: "not until") = **erst** (*Il n'arrive qu'à 10 h → Er kommt **erst** um 10 Uhr*); quantity "only" = **nur**.

### A2-05
Your French *être / avoir* intuition covers 90 % of German `sein / haben` in the Perfekt! Watch the **three differences** where French and German diverge:
1. **All reflexive verbs** take *être* in French (*Je me suis inscrit / reposé*), but **always `haben`** in German (*Ich **habe** mich angemeldet / ausgeruht*)!
2. **`sein` itself** takes *avoir* in French (*J'ai été malade*), but **`sein`** in German (*Ich **bin** krank **gewesen***).
3. *courir, nager, sauter, voler (avion)* take *avoir* in French, but **`sein`** in German when moving from A to B (*Ich **bin** gelaufen / geschwommen / geflogen*).

### A2-06
Just as French reserves *passé simple* (*il alla, ils virent*) for written literature/history, German uses the **Präteritum** of full verbs (*er ging, sie sahen, er kam*) mainly in written narrative (newspaper articles, stories — key for Lesen!). However, for `sein, haben, werden`, the modals, `wissen` and `es gab`, German uses Präteritum **everywhere** (speech, e-mails, letters), where French would use *imparfait* or *passé composé*.

### A2-07
Watch two big French → German modal traps:
1. French *pouvoir* covers both ability and permission. German splits them: **können** = ability/possibility (*Je sais/peux nager → Ich **kann** schwimmen*); **dürfen** = permission (*Puis-je entrer ? → **Darf** ich reinkommen?*).
2. In French, *Tu ne dois pas fumer ici* means prohibition ("must not"). In German, **nicht müssen** means *"tu n'es pas obligé de"* (not necessary); prohibition is always **nicht dürfen** (*Du **darfst** hier **nicht** rauchen*).

### A2-08
In French, most descriptive adjectives stand **after** the noun (*une voiture rouge, un livre intéressant*) and agree only in gender and number. In German, attributive adjectives **always stand before the noun** and carry a case ending. After `der/die/das` (and `dieser, jeder, welcher, alle`), only two endings exist: **`-e`** in the 5 basic cells (`●` m/n/f, `▲` n/f) and **`-en`** everywhere else.

### A2-09
Remember the "signal once" principle: after `ein / kein / mein`, the three cells where `ein` has **no ending** (`●` m *ein*, `●` n *ein*, `▲` n *ein*) force the adjective to show the gender signal itself (**ein alt`er` Mann, ein klein`es` Kind**). Also note that French uses *de / des* before plural adjectives (*de bons amis*); German uses **zero article + strong Rule-C ending** (*gut**e** Freunde, mit gut**en** Freunden*).

### A2-10
Where French forms the comparative and superlative analytically with *plus / le plus* (*plus grand, le plus rapide*), German always adds **`-er`** and **`am -sten / der -ste`** directly to the adjective — **Even on long adjectives** (*interessanter, am interessantesten* — never ~~mehr interessant~~!). For comparisons: French *plus … **que*** = German **Komparativ + als** (*größer **als***); French *aussi … **que*** = German **(genau)so … wie** (*so groß **wie***).

### A2-11
In French, *que, parce que, quand, si* keep normal `Subject + Verb + Object` order (*Je pense qu'il **vient** demain*). In German, `dass, weil, wenn` push the conjugated verb to the **very end** of the clause, preceded by an **obligatory comma** (*Ich glaube**,** dass er morgen **kommt***). When the subordinate clause comes first, the main verb follows immediately after the comma: *Quand j'ai le temps, je lis → Wenn ich Zeit habe, **lese ich**.*

### A2-12
French uses ***si*** for two different things: (1) a condition (*Si j'ai le temps* → **wenn / falls**), and (2) an indirect yes/no question (*Je ne sais pas **s'**il vient* → **ob**!). Never use *wenn* for indirect "whether" (*Ich weiß nicht, **ob** er kommt*). For purpose ("in order to"), French uses *pour + infinitif* (same subject) vs *pour que + subjonctif* (different subject); German uses **um … zu + Infinitiv** (same subject) vs **damit + Indikativ V-END** (different subject — no subjunctive needed!).

### A2-13
French *conditionnel présent* (*j'aurais, je serais, je pourrais, je devrais, je voudrais / j'aimerais + infinitif*) maps directly to German **Konjunktiv II**: *j'aurais → ich **hätte***, *je serais → ich **wäre***, *je pourrais → ich **könnte***, *je devrais → ich **sollte***, and for all other verbs **würde + Infinitiv** (*j'achèterais → ich **würde … kaufen***). Use them in every B1 letter for polite requests (*Pourriez-vous… → **Könnten** Sie bitte…*).

### A2-14
Like French (*se dépêcher → sich beeilen, se reposer → sich ausruhen, se souvenir de → sich erinnern an*), German has many reflexive verbs. Watch three differences:
1. When there is a direct object (body part / clothing), the reflexive pronoun is **`■` Dativ** (*Je me lave les mains → Ich wasche **mir** die Hände*).
2. In the Perfekt, all German reflexives take **`haben`**, never *sein* (*Je me suis trompé → Ich **habe** mich geirrt*)!
3. Prepositions never translate 1:1: *attendre qn/qc (no prep!) → **warten auf ▲***, *penser à → **denken an ▲***, *s'intéresser à → **sich interessieren für ▲***, *avoir peur de → **Angst haben vor ■***, *rêver de → **träumen von ■***.

### A2-15
In French, you choose between ***qui*** (subject = `●`) and ***que*** (direct object = `▲`) regardless of gender (*l'homme **qui** habite ici / l'homme **que** je vois*). You can use that exact French *qui / que* test to find the German case (`qui` → `● der/das/die`, `que` → `▲ den/das/die`), then pick the gender and number from the noun before the comma (*der Mann, **der** [qui] hier wohnt / der Mann, **den** [que] ich sehe*)! Don't forget the comma and verb-final order.

### A2-16
In French, linking adverbs (*c'est pourquoi, pourtant, cependant, ensuite, de plus*) are followed by `Subject + Verb` (*Pourtant, il vient*). In German, `deshalb, trotzdem, dann, außerdem, sonst` sit in **Position 1** and force the verb into **Position 2** before the subject (*Trotzdem **kommt er*** — never ~~Trotzdem er kommt~~).

### A2-17
Where French uses *le mien / la mienne* (*C'est le mien*), German uses standalone possessives with strong `der`-endings (*Das ist **meiner** [m] / **meins** [n] / **meine** [f]*). And look at French **`en`** for indefinite quantities (*Tu as des pommes ? — Oui, j'**en** ai / Tu veux un stylo ? — Oui, j'**en** veux un*): German expresses French *en* with the standalone indefinite pronoun **einen / eins / eine** (singular) or **welche** (plural: *Ja, ich habe **welche***)!

### A2-18
In French, *chaque* (*jeder*) is singular and *tous les* (*alle*) is plural — same in German (*jedes Kind, alle Kinder*). The big contrast is the **French partitive and plural indefinite article** (*Je bois **du** lait, je mange **de la** soupe, j'ai **des** amis, j'ai **beaucoup d'**amis*): German uses **no article / no `de`** at all (*Ich trinke Milch, ich habe Freunde, ich habe **viele** Freunde / **viel** Zeit*)!

### A2-19
`werden` has three jobs that correspond to three different French structures:
1. Full verb = **devenir** (*Il devient médecin → Er **wird** Arzt; il fait froid → es **wird** kalt*).
2. Future (`werden + Infinitiv`) = *futur simple* (*Je t'aiderai → Ich **werde** dir **helfen***), though for planned future with a time word German prefers **Präsens** (*Demain je pars → Morgen **fahre** ich*).
3. Passive (`werden + Partizip II`) = *être + participe passé* (*La lettre est écrite → Der Brief **wird** geschrieben*).

### A2-20
As seen in A2-01, French 3rd-person clitic order (*Je **le lui** donne*) is the exact same as German two-pronoun order (`▲` pronoun **before** `■` pronoun: *Ich gebe **es ihm***). Just remember that German applies `▲ before ■` to **all** persons (*Il **me le** donne → Er gibt **es mir***)! For adverbials, French often puts place right after the verb (*Je vais **à Berlin** demain*); German follows **Te-Ka-Mo-Lo** (Time → Reason → Manner → Place: *Ich fahre **morgen mit dem Zug nach Berlin***).

### A2-21
Compare French **`y`** and **`en`** (or *à quoi / de quoi*) with German **`da(r)-`** and **`wo(r)-`**: For **things**, French uses *J'**y** pense / J'**en** parle / **À quoi** penses-tu ?*; German fuses **`da(r)-` / `wo(r)-` + the verb's exact preposition**: *Ich denke **daran** / Ich spreche **darüber** / **Woran** denkst du?* · For **people**, both French and German keep the preposition + personal/question pronoun: ***À qui** penses-tu ? — Je pense **à elle*** = ***An wen** denkst du? — Ich denke **an sie***.

### B1-01
French *de / du / de la / des* maps directly to the German `◆ Genitiv`: *le bureau **du** chef → das Büro **des** Chefs*; *à cause **de la** pluie → wegen **des** Regens*; *malgré **le** froid → trotz **der** Kälte*; *pendant **les** vacances → während **des** Urlaubs*. Remember that masculine and neuter nouns add **`-(e)s`** on both the article and the noun (*des Chef**s**, des Kind**es***).

### B1-02
After any Genitiv article (*des, eines, meines, dieser*), the adjective ending is always **`-en`** (*wegen des schlecht**en** Wetters*). For nominalised adjectives (*un Allemand, un employé, un proche, les jeunes*), French turns them into regular nouns, whereas German declines them as **adjectives** (`der Bekannt**e**` vs `ein Bekannt**er**` vs `viele Bekannt**e**`). And French *quelque chose **de** nouveau / rien **de** grave / tout **le** meilleur* takes no preposition in German: **etwas Neues, nichts Schlimmes, alles Gute**.

### B1-03
In French, you constantly choose between *passé composé* (completed event) and *imparfait* (background/habit) in both speech and writing. German does **not** split past tenses by aspect! In German, **Präteritum** (*er kam, sie sagte*) and **Perfekt** (*er ist gekommen, sie hat gesagt*) mean the exact same thing; Präteritum is simply the narrative tense of newspaper articles and stories (plus `war/hatte/konnte` everywhere).

### B1-04
French uses *quand* for all three German words: pause and choose **als** for a single event/period in the past (*Quand j'étais enfant → **Als** ich ein Kind war*), **wenn** for repeated past (*chaque fois que → immer **wenn***) or present/future (*quand tu viendras → **wenn** du kommst*), and **wann** for direct/indirect questions (*Je ne sais pas quand il vient → …, **wann** er kommt*). The Plusquamperfekt matches French *plus-que-parfait* (*j'avais mangé / j'étais parti → ich **hatte** gegessen / ich **war** abgefahren*), used after **nachdem** (*après que*).

### B1-05
In French, a future time clause requires the future tense (*Quand je **serai** à la maison, je t'**appellerai***). In German, both clauses normally stay in the **Präsens** (*Wenn ich zu Hause **bin**, **rufe** ich dich **an***)! On the other hand, just like French uses the *futur antérieur / futur simple* for present conjecture (*Il sera encore au bureau* = "He must still be at the office"), German uses **`werden + (wohl) + Infinitiv`** for present assumptions (*Er **wird wohl** noch im Büro **sein***).

### B1-06
Look at the tense difference in unreal *if*-sentences: Present unreal: French uses *Si + **imparfait*** in the *si*-clause and ***conditionnel*** in the main clause (*Si j'**avais** le temps, je **viendrais***). German puts **Konjunktiv II in BOTH clauses**: *Wenn ich Zeit **hätte**, **würde** ich **kommen*** · Past unreal: French uses *Si + **plus-que-parfait*** and ***conditionnel passé*** (*Si j'**avais su**, je **serais venu***). German puts **`hätte / wäre + Partizip II` in BOTH clauses**: *Wenn ich das **gewusst hätte**, **wäre** ich **gekommen***.

### B1-07
This is a major French → German trap: French forms the passive with ***être* + participe passé** (*La voiture **est** réparée / **a été** réparée*). If you translate *est / était / a été* with `ist / war`, you get the **state passive** (already finished)! For an **action** (process passive), always use **`werden`**: *Das Auto **wird** repariert (est réparée) / **wurde** repariert (a été réparée) / **ist** repariert **worden***. French ***on*** (*On répare la voiture*) maps directly to German **`man`** (*Man repariert das Auto*).

### B1-08
French *pour / sans / au lieu de + infinitif* (*pour aider, sans rien dire, au lieu d'attendre*) maps directly to **`um … zu / ohne … zu / (an)statt … zu + Infinitiv`**, with the exact same rule: only when the subject of both clauses is the **same**! (When subjects differ: *pour que → **damit***, *sans que → **ohne dass***). And French ***faire + infinitif*** (*Je **fais réparer** ma voiture*) is expressed with **`lassen + Infinitiv`** (*Ich **lasse** mein Auto **reparieren*** — no `zu`!).

### B1-09
Two great simplifications compared to French:
1. In French, *bien que (obwohl), jusqu'à ce que (bis), pour que (damit), sans que (ohne dass)* require the **subjonctif** (*bien qu'il **soit** malade*). In German, **all** of these take the normal **Indikativ** (with the verb at the end: *obwohl er krank **ist***)!
2. French **`en + participe présent`** (gérondif of manner/means: *On économise de l'énergie **en éteignant** la lumière*) translates into German as **`indem + subject + verb at end`** (*…, **indem man** das Licht **ausschaltet***).

### B1-10
Map the six two-part connectors to French: *aussi bien … que / tant … que* → **sowohl … als auch** · *non seulement …, mais aussi* → **nicht nur …, sondern auch** · *soit … soit / ou … ou* → **entweder … oder** · *ni … ni* → **weder … noch** (Note: unlike French *Je **n'**ai **ni** temps **ni** envie*, German adds **no extra negation** — *Ich habe **weder** Zeit **noch** Lust*!) · *certes / il est vrai que …, mais* → **zwar …, aber** · *plus …, plus …* (*Plus je lis, mieux je comprends*) → **je + comparative (+ V-END!), desto + comparative (+ V2!)** (*Je mehr ich **lese**, desto besser **verstehe ich***).

### B1-11
In French, connectors from all three categories (*car, parce que, c'est pourquoi / mais, bien que, pourtant*) keep `Subject + Verb` order after them. In German, choosing between **denn / weil / deshalb** or **aber / obwohl / trotzdem** in Sprachbausteine is a **word-order test first**: verb at the end → Subjunktor (`weil, da, obwohl`); verb right after the gap → Adverb (`deshalb, trotzdem, sonst`); subject + verb after the gap → Konjunktor (`denn, aber, sondern`).

### B1-12
Compare French relative pronouns with German: French *à qui / auquel / auxquels* (COI) → **`■` dem / der / denen** (*les voisins **à qui** je parle → die Nachbarn, **mit denen** ich spreche*) · French ***dont*** ("whose / of which") splits in German! When *dont* means possession ("whose": *l'homme **dont** la voiture… / la femme **dont** le fils…*), use **`dessen`** (m/n owner) or **`deren`** (f/pl owner) + noun **without article** (*der Mann, **dessen** Auto… / die Frau, **deren** Sohn…*). When *dont* comes from a verb with *de* (*le livre **dont** je t'ai parlé*), use the German verb's preposition + relative pronoun (*das Buch, **von dem** ich dir erzählt habe*)! · French ***ce qui / ce que*** (*tout **ce que** tu dis*) = German **`was`** (*alles, **was** du sagst*).

### B1-13
In French, you can write *Je me réjouis **de** venir* or *Je veille **à ce que** tout soit prêt*. In German, whenever a prepositional verb (*sich freuen auf, sich bedanken für, achten auf, abhängen von*) is followed by a `dass/ob` clause or `zu + Infinitiv`, you put the matching **`da(r)-` compound** (*darauf, dafür, davon, darüber, darum*) in the main clause as a bridge: *Ich freue mich **darauf**, Sie **zu sehen** / Ich bedanke mich **dafür**, **dass** Sie mir geholfen haben*.

### B1-14
Big relief for French speakers: French has strict *concordance des temps* after a past reporting verb (*Il a dit qu'il **était** malade / qu'il **viendrait***). German does **not** shift the tense backwards! After *Er sagte* (past), you keep the present tense of the original thought: *Er sagte, dass er krank **ist** (or news Konjunktiv I: **sei**)*. When reading news texts, recognise `er sei` (*il serait / est, dit-on*), `er habe`, `es gebe`, and plural `sie hätten / würden` as neutral reported speech.

### B1-15
In French media, an unconfirmed report is expressed with the *conditionnel journalistique* (*Le nouveau restaurant **serait** excellent / Deux personnes **auraient** été blessées*). German expresses this exact "hearsay / reportedly" meaning with **`sollen` in the Präsens**: *Das neue Restaurant **soll** sehr gut **sein** / Zwei Personen **sollen** verletzt worden **sein***. And French *Tu n'as pas besoin de venir* = **Du brauchst nicht zu kommen** (= *Du musst nicht kommen*).

### B1-16
Many abstract nouns correspond cleanly: French `-tion / -ment` ≈ German **`-ung`** (always `die`: *die Reservierung, die Erklärung*); French `-té / -esse` ≈ German **`-heit / -keit`** (always `die`: *die Möglichkeit = la possibilité, die Freiheit = la liberté*); French `-able / -ible` = German **`-bar`** (*lösbar, erreichbar*); French *sans + noun* = **`-los`** (*kostenlos = gratuit / sans frais*). French *en lisant / pendant la lecture* = **`beim Lesen`** (`bei dem` + capitalised infinitive!).

### B1-17
French uses *le même / la même* for both one shared object and two identical objects. German distinguishes **derselbe / dasselbe / dieselbe** (one single shared item: *Nous habitons dans le même immeuble → im **selben** Haus*) from **der / das / die gleiche** (an identical model: *Tu as la même veste que moi → **die gleiche** Jacke*). French *l'un avec l'autre / ensemble* fuses into **miteinander** (*füreinander, voneinander*). And French *Il s'agit de…* = **Es handelt sich um + ▲** / **Es geht um + ▲** (never drop `es`!).

### B1-18
Map the French negative pairs directly to German (with **no `ne`**!): And for two object pronouns (*Je **le lui** donne*), remember that German always puts the `▲` pronoun (`ihn, es, sie`) **before** the `■` pronoun (`mir, dir, ihm, ihr, Ihnen`): *Ich gebe **es ihm** / **es dir***. *déjà (schon)* ↔ *pas encore* = **noch nicht** (with a noun: *pas encore de réponse* = **noch keine** Antwort) · *déjà (dans ta vie: schon einmal)* ↔ *ne … jamais* = **noch nie** · *encore (noch)* ↔ *ne … plus* = **nicht mehr** (with a noun: *Je n'ai **plus de** temps* = *Ich habe **keine** Zeit **mehr*** — wrap `kein- … mehr` around the noun!).

### B1-19
French *participe présent* (`-ant`: *les enfants souriants, la semaine suivante*) matches German **Partizip I** (`Infinitiv + -d + adjective ending`: *die lächelnd**en** Kinder, in der folgend**en** Woche*), and French *participe passé* (*le contrat signé*) matches **Partizip II + adjective ending** (*der unterschrieben**e** Vertrag*). Crucial reading difference: French puts extended participial phrases **after** the noun (*le courrier **envoyé hier par votre service***); German packs the entire phrase **between the article and the noun** (*der **gestern von Ihnen geschickte** Brief*).

### B1-20
Compare key B1 prepositions with French: *dans un délai de 14 jours* → **innerhalb von 14 Tagen** (*innerhalb/außerhalb* + `◆`: *außerhalb der Öffnungszeiten* = outside opening hours) · *sauf moi / à part mon frère* → **außer mir / außer meinem Bruder** (`■` Dativ!) · *en face de la gare* → **gegenüber dem Bahnhof** (or *dem Bahnhof gegenüber*, `■`) · *le long du fleuve* → **den Fluss entlang** (`▲`, *entlang* stands **after** the noun!) · *à mon avis / selon moi* → **meiner Meinung nach** (*nach* stands **after** *Meinung*!) · *à la campagne* → **auf dem Land** (direction: **aufs Land**); *à la plage* → **am Strand** (direction: **an den Strand**).

### B1-21
Watch three strict differences between French and German formal letters:
1. **Salutation & first line:** In French, you write *Madame, Monsieur,* and start the next paragraph with a **capital** letter (*Je vous écris…*). In German, after *Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,* the first word of the body starts **lowercase** (*ich schreibe Ihnen… / vielen Dank…*) unless it is a noun or `Sie`!
2. **Closing formula:** Instead of long French closings (*Je vous prie d'agréer…*), German uses concise **Mit freundlichen Grüßen** with **no comma** after it.
3. **Capital `Sie / Ihnen / Ihr`:** Unlike French *vous / votre* (lowercase), formal `Sie, Ihnen, Ihr-` is always capitalised.
