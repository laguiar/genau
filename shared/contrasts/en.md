# Language Pack — English (`en`)

**Code:** `en` · **Language:** English · **Flag:** 🇬🇧 · **Label:** English contrast

Master reference for English contrasts and L2 transfer traps when learning German up to telc B1. `python3 review/review.py lang sync` uses the `## Topic notes` section below to populate the `> 🇬🇧 **English contrast**` boxes across `A1/`, `A2/`, and `B1/`.

## Key English → German Transfer Rules

| German feature | English transfer | Contrast note & key traps |
|---|---|---|
| Three grammatical genders (`der/die/das`) | misleads (no grammatical gender) | Never map all inanimate objects to `es`; *der Tisch → er*, *die Lampe → sie*, *das Buch → es*. |
| Noun capitalisation | misleads | Every German noun is capitalised (*das Haus, das Lesen*), while nationality adjectives are lowercase (*ein deutscher Wein*). |
| No continuous tense (`-ing`) | misleads | Never translate *I am working / I am going* with `sein`: *Ich arbeite (gerade) / Ich gehe*. |
| V2 inversion in main clauses | misleads | Fronting an adverb or clause forces the subject behind the verb: *Tomorrow I travel* → *Morgen **fahre ich***. |
| Verb-final in subordinate clauses | misleads | *weil, dass, wenn, ob, obwohl* push the conjugated verb to the end: *…, weil ich keine Zeit **habe***. |
| `nicht müssen` vs `nicht dürfen` | misleads | English *must not* (prohibition) = **nicht dürfen**; German *nicht müssen* = *don't have to*. |
| `seit` + Präsens | misleads | English *I have lived here for 3 years* (present perfect) = German **Präsens**: *Ich **wohne** seit drei Jahren hier*. |
| `wer` vs `wo` / `also` / `bekommen` / `Gift` | misleads (false friends) | *wer* = who, *wo* = where; *also* = so/therefore; *bekommen* = receive; *das Gift* = poison. |
| Passiv (`werden` vs `sein`) | misleads | English uses *to be* for both action and state; German uses **wird/wurde gebaut** (action) vs **ist gebaut** (state). |

## Topic notes

### A1-01
English capitalises days, months and languages but not nouns; German capitalises days and months **because they are nouns** and *Deutsch* as a language, but not *deutsch* as an adjective.

### A1-02
English speakers under-conjugate (*er wohn* instead of *er wohnt*) and try to build *I am learning* with *sein* (*ich bin lernen*) — wrong. One present tense only.

### A1-03
*I am hungry / cold / 30 years old* → German uses **haben**: *Ich habe Hunger, ich habe kalt* (colloquial: *mir ist kalt*), *ich bin 30 (Jahre alt)* — age does use *sein*.

### A1-04
No *do/does*. *Do you live here?* → *Wohnst du hier?* And no *-ing*: *Where are you going?* → *Wohin gehst du?*

### A1-05
English has one *the* and one *a*. German has three of each and they change with case; the article carries most of the grammar of the noun phrase. Do not skip it.

### A1-06
Same trap: *-s* is not the default. But English keeps a few German-style plurals (*man/men, foot/feet, child/children*) that show the umlaut and *-er* idea.

### A1-07
*not a / not any / no* all become *kein*: *I have no time / I don't have any time* → *Ich habe keine Zeit*. English *don't* disappears: the main verb takes *nicht* after it.

### A1-08
*him* = *ihn* (m), but *it* = *es* (n) or *ihn* (m) or *sie* (f) depending on the noun's gender: *Der Tisch? Ich kaufe **ihn**. Das Buch? Ich kaufe **es**. Die Lampe? Ich kaufe **sie**.*

### A1-09
*his/her* choice is identical to *sein/ihr*, but English adds no endings; German does. *its* = *sein-* (neuter owner: *das Kind und sein Spielzeug*).

### A1-10
Phrasal verbs (*get up, call back, pick up*) are the model: *I pick my friend up* → *Ich hole meinen Freund ab*. English can also keep them together (*pick up my friend*); German main clauses cannot.

### A1-11
*I must not* means prohibition; German *ich muss nicht* means "I don't have to" (A2-07). *want to* → *wollen/möchten* + infinitive without *zu*: *Ich will gehen* (no *zu*).

### A1-12
One imperative for everyone; German has three. *Let's go* = *Gehen wir!* / *Lass uns gehen!*

### A1-13
*at 8* = *um 8*; *on Monday* = *am Montag*; *in May* = *im Mai*; *half past eight* = *halb neun*; *a quarter to* = *Viertel vor*. 24-hour clock is normal in writing.

### A1-14
*to* splits into *nach* (places without article), *zu* (people, functions), *in* + ▲ (into buildings). *at the doctor's* = *beim Arzt*; *by bus* = *mit dem Bus*.

### A1-15
*help, thank, answer, believe, congratulate* take a plain object in English; in German they are ■. *I like the book* → *Das Buch gefällt mir* (the book is the subject).

### A1-16
*I have eaten* looks the same, but German Perfekt also means *I ate* (finished, yesterday). And English *I am gone* is archaic; German *ich bin gegangen* is the only way. Irregular participles overlap a little (*gesungen / sung, getrunken / drunk*).

### A1-17
*was/were, had, could, had to, wanted (to)* map one to one. *could* in the sense of "would be able" is Konjunktiv II *könnte* (A2-13), not *konnte*.

### A1-18
Drop the *-ly*. *I like to swim* → *Ich schwimme gern* (no *mögen* + infinitive). *very* = *sehr*, *a lot* = *viel*, *too* = *zu*.

### A1-19
*Then I go* → *Dann gehe ich* (verb before subject). *for* (= because) ≈ *denn*.

### A1-20
*it* is three words in German (*er/es/sie*). *one/you/they* generic → *man*. *something nice* → *etwas Nettes* (capital, ending).

### A2-01
*I gave the boy the book* mirrors *dem Jungen das Buch*; *I gave it to him* mirrors *es ihm*. The traps are the ■ verbs with no "to": *help, thank, answer, follow, believe, trust*.

### A2-02
*put* splits four ways; *be* splits into *stand/lie/sit/hang* — English has these verbs but rarely insists on them. *in/into, on/onto* is the closest English has to the ■/▲ contrast.

### A2-03
*for* = *für* only for benefit/purpose/duration; *for three years* (still going) = *seit drei Jahren*. *by bus* = *mit dem Bus*. *at 8* = *um 8*; *at my aunt's* = *bei meiner Tante*.

### A2-04
*for two years* (still) = *seit*, (finished) = *zwei Jahre (lang)*; *since* = *seit*; *ago* = *vor* (before the number!); *in an hour* = *in einer Stunde*; *until* = *bis*; *from … on* = *ab*.

### A2-05
English never uses *be* as a past auxiliary. Test: if you can ask "where to?", it is *sein*. English prefix cognates: *understand → verstehen, forget → vergessen, begin → beginnen* — all inseparable, no *ge-*.

### A2-06
The Präteritum is the *I went / I saw* form and many strong verbs rhyme with English (*kam – came, sah – saw, trank – drank, sang – sang, fand – found, begann – began*). Use these as hooks.

### A2-07
*may/be allowed to* = *dürfen*; *must not* = *nicht dürfen*; *don't have to / needn't* = *nicht müssen*; *should / be supposed to* = *sollen*.

### A2-08
Position is the same as English (*the old man*); the ending is new. English speakers tend to write *der alt Mann* — no ending at all is always wrong before a noun.

### A2-09
*a good wine* / *good wine* / *the good wine*: three different German endings (*ein guter Wein, guter Wein, der gute Wein*).

### A2-10
Never *more/most* + adjective. *than* → *als* (not *wie*). *the best* attributive vs *best* predicative → *der beste* vs *am besten*.

### A2-11
Same as PT: English keeps verb after subject. English *when* = *wenn* (repeated/future) or *als* (single past); *if* = *wenn* (or *falls*). English can omit *that*; German prefers the *dass*-clause in writing.

### A2-12
*if* is *ob* in indirect questions (*I don't know if he's coming*), *wenn* only in conditions. *in order to* = *um … zu*; *so that* = *damit*.

### A2-13
*would* = *würde*, *could* = *könnte*, *should* = *sollte*, *would have* = *hätte*, *would be* = *wäre*. *I'd like* = *Ich hätte gern / Ich möchte* (not *Ich würde mögen*).

### A2-14
*wait for* → *warten auf*; *think of/about* → *denken an*; *be interested in* → *sich interessieren für*; *be afraid of* → *Angst haben vor*; *look forward to* → *sich freuen auf*; *apply for* → *sich bewerben um*. Never translate the English preposition.

### A2-15
*who/whom/which/that* → *der/das/die*; English can drop the pronoun (*the man I saw*), German cannot: *der Mann, **den** ich gesehen habe.*

### A2-16
*therefore I stay* → *deshalb bleibe ich*. *also* ≠ *also*: German *also* = so/therefore; English *also* = *auch/außerdem*.

### A2-17
*mine/yours* → *meins/deins* with case endings; *one/none* → *einer/keiner*; *some* (pl.) → *welche*; *which* → *welcher*, *what kind of* → *was für ein*.

### A2-18
*I am a teacher* → *Ich bin Lehrer* (no *ein*). *many/much* → *viele/viel*; *a few* → *ein paar*; *a little* → *etwas / ein bisschen*; *every* → *jeder*; *all* → *alle*.

### A2-19
*will* ≠ *werden* in most everyday sentences; *I'll call you tomorrow* → *Ich rufe dich morgen an*. *get* (become) → *werden*: *it's getting cold* → *es wird kalt*. *to be + past participle* (passive) → *werden + Partizip II*, not *sein*.

### A2-20
*I gave it to him* → *Ich gab es ihm* (no "to"). *I didn't see him yesterday* → *Ich habe ihn gestern nicht gesehen* (*nicht* after the time). English adverbial order (manner–place–time) is nearly the mirror of German (time–manner–place).

### A2-21
*What are you waiting for?* → *Worauf wartest du?* (one word, preposition first). *Who are you talking to?* → *Mit wem sprichst du?* *thank for* → *sich bedanken für*; *ask for* → *bitten um*; *take part in* → *teilnehmen an ■*; *depend on* → *abhängen von*.

### B1-01
English `'s` only matches German proper names (*Anna's brother* = *Annas Bruder*, no apostrophe!). For common nouns, follow the English *of*-phrase order: *the colour **of the car*** → *die Farbe **des Autos***.

### B1-02
English says *the rich, the unemployed* (plural, no ending) or needs *one* (*a new one*). German inflects for singular and plural: *der Arbeitslose* (one man), *die Arbeitslose* (one woman), *die Arbeitslosen* (plural).

### B1-03
Do not map German Präteritum to English Simple Past (*I went*) and Perfekt to Present Perfect (*I have gone*). *Gestern habe ich ihn gesehen* (spoken/letter) and *Gestern sah ich ihn* (written story) both mean *Yesterday I saw him*.

### B1-04
English *before / after / while* can be prepositions (*after dinner, before Monday*) OR conjunctions (*after I ate, before I go*). German strictly separates them: before a **noun** (preposition): **nach ■** (*nach dem Essen*), **vor ■** (*vor dem Montag*), **während ◆** (*während des Essens*). - before a **clause** (conjunction, verb at end): **nachdem** (*nachdem ich gegessen hatte*), **bevor** (*bevor ich gehe*), **während** (*während ich esse*). Never write ~~nach ich gegessen habe~~ or ~~vor ich gehe~~!

### B1-05
English requires *will* or *going to* for future facts (*Tomorrow I will call you*). German Präsens (*Morgen rufe ich dich an*) is fully natural and grammatical. Conversely, *Er wird wohl krank sein* means *He is probably ill (now)*, not in the future.

### B1-06
English uses Simple Past in the *if*-clause (*If I **had** money, I **would** buy it*). Do not use German Präteritum (*hatte / war*) for unreal conditions: *Wenn ich Geld **hatte*** means "Whenever I had money (in the past, real)"; *Wenn ich Geld **hätte*** means "If I had money (now, unreal)".

### B1-07
Same trap: English uses *to be* (*is built, was built*) for both action and state. In German, action = **wird / wurde gebaut**; state = **ist gebaut**.

### B1-08
English uses `-ing` after *without* and *instead of* (*without saying, instead of waiting*), and allows *want you to do*: ~~Ich will dich zu kommen~~ does NOT exist in German → *Ich möchte, **dass du kommst***.

### B1-09
English uses *by + -ing* (*by pressing the button*), *since + -ing / clause*, and *until*. In German, **indem** always requires a full clause with a subject and a conjugated verb at the end: *…, **indem Sie** auf den Knopf **drücken***.

### B1-10
English *the sooner you book, the cheaper the tickets are* keeps subject-before-verb in both halves. German flips both: *Je früher du **buchst** (V-END), desto billiger **sind** die Tickets (V2)*.

### B1-11
English *because / although / therefore / however* all keep `Subject + Verb`. In German, *deshalb* and *trotzdem* behave like fronted adverbs and force **Verb + Subject** (*Deshalb **bleibe ich***, never ~~Deshalb ich bleibe~~).

### B1-12
English often puts the preposition at the end of the relative clause and drops the pronoun (*the flat I told you **about***). German **never** strands a preposition at the end and never drops the pronoun: *die Wohnung, **von der** ich dir erzählt habe*.

### B1-13
English cannot say *I look forward to that you come*; it uses *to + -ing* (*to your coming / to seeing you*). German uses *Ich freue mich **darauf**, **dass** du kommst* or *Ich freue mich **darauf**, dich **zu sehen***.

### B1-14
English *He said he **was** tired* → German *Er sagte, dass er müde **ist** (or **sei**)*. And remember that an indirect question (*Er fragte, wann der Zug **kommt***) never has a question mark when the main clause is a statement.

### B1-15
English *The film is supposed to be good* matches *Der Film soll gut sein*. Watch out for *must not* vs *can't*: for negative deduction ("That can't be right!"), both English and German use **kann nicht** (*Das kann nicht stimmen*).

### B1-16
English writes compounds as separate words (*language course registration form*). German glues them into one word (*das Sprachkursanmeldeformular*). Always find the **last** word first to know the gender and what the thing actually is.

### B1-17
English also uses *the same* for both *derselbe* and *der gleiche*. And where English says *I find **it** hard to believe*, German uses the same placeholder *es*: *Ich finde **es** schwer zu glauben*.

### B1-18
English *I don't have any more time* / *any longer* uses *not … any*. German fuses *not + a/any* into **kein-**: *Ich habe **kein** Geld **mehr*** (never ~~nicht mehr Geld~~ when you mean "no money left"; *nicht mehr Geld* means "not a larger amount of money").

### B1-19
English `-ing` before a noun (*the sleeping child, rising prices*) matches German Partizip I (`-end`). English past participle (*the stolen car*) matches Partizip II (`gestohlen-`). Unlike English (*the letter sent yesterday*), German puts the whole phrase **before** the noun (*der gestern geschickte Brief*).

### B1-20
English *except me / besides him* matches *außer mir / außer ihm* (■). English *in the countryside* uses *in*; German uses **auf dem Land** (*im Land* means "in the country/nation").

### B1-21
English capitalises every word in *Dear Mr. Smith,* and puts a comma after *Best regards,*. German writes *Sehr **g**eehrter Herr Schmidt,* (lowercase *geehrter*!) and **no comma** after *Mit freundlichen Grüßen*.
