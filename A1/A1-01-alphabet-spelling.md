## 1. The rule in one picture

```
 LONG vowel  → one consonant / h / doubled vowel      Stra-ße, wohnen, Boot, Liebe (ie = long i)
 SHORT vowel → double consonant / ss / ck / tz         essen, kommen, Fluss, Zucker, sitzen

 ie  = "ee"   →  Liebe, vier, Spiel          (think: L-EE-be)
 ei  = "eye"  →  mein, drei, Arbeit          (think: m-EYE-n)

 ä ö ü  are LETTERS  (a→ä changes meaning: Apfel / Äpfel, schon / schön)
 ß      = "ss" after a LONG vowel or au/ei/eu:  Straße, heißen, groß, außen
 ss     = "ss" after a SHORT vowel:             Fluss, essen, Schloss, muss

 CAPITAL: every noun · sentence start · Sie/Ihnen/Ihr (formal you)
 lowercase: adjectives, verbs, du/dich/dir (even in letters), nationality adjectives (deutsch)
```

## 2. The rule in words

**Nouns are capitalised.** All of them, always: *das Haus, die Liebe, das Lesen* (a verb used as a noun). Adjectives derived from countries are lowercase (*ein deutscher Freund*), but the language and the country are nouns (*Deutsch, Deutschland*). This is the check you do **last** on every text.

**`ß` vs `ss`.** Both spell the sharp "s". After a **long** vowel or a diphthong (`au, ei, eu`), write `ß`: *Straße, Fuß, heißen, groß, weiß, draußen*. After a **short** vowel, write `ss`: *essen, Fluss, muss, Schloss, Wasser*. If you cannot hear the length, memorise the word; the exam does not test the theory, only the word.

**Double consonants mark a short vowel.** *kommen* (short o) vs *Kohle* (long o); *bitte* (short i) vs *Biete* (long). Related spellings: `ck` = double k (*Zucker*), `tz` = double z (*sitzen*).

**Long vowels** are shown by `h` (*wohnen, fahren, Uhr*), by doubling (*Boot, Meer, Staat*), or by `ie` for long i (*Liebe, vier*). Otherwise a vowel followed by **one** consonant is usually long (*Name, Weg, gut*).

**`ie` vs `ei`** — say the second letter's *English* name: `ie` → "e" → "ee" (*Liebe*); `ei` → "i" → "eye" (*mein*).

**Umlauts** are separate letters: `ä ö ü`. Alphabetical order treats them as `a o u`. If a keyboard has none, write `ae oe ue` (and `ss` for ß); never drop the dots.

**Formal `Sie`** and every form of it is capitalised in letters: *Sie, Ihnen, Ihr Brief, Ihre Anzeige*. Informal *du, dich, dir, dein* are lowercase (an old rule allowed capitals in letters; the current standard is lowercase).

**Punctuation you need:**
- Comma **before every subordinate clause** (*dass, weil, wenn, ob, W-word*) and before relative clauses: *Ich weiß**,** dass du kommst. Das Buch**,** das ich lese**,** ist gut.*
- Comma between two main clauses joined by `aber, denn, sondern`; optional before `und/oder` (leave it out).
- Comma after the salutation in a letter, then lowercase: *Liebe Anna**,** vielen Dank …*
- No comma before `und` in a list: *Brot, Käse und Wein*.
- Numbers: decimal **comma**, thousands **point**: *3,50 €* · *1.000 €*. Dates: *7.9.2026* or *7. September 2026*.
- Quotation marks in German print are „low-high“; in the exam, "straight" quotes are fine.

> 🇧🇷 **Portuguese contrast** — Portuguese never capitalises common nouns and has no double-consonant length rule, so both errors are automatic for a PT speaker. Also: German has **no accents** (*á, ã, ç*) and `ç`-like "ss" sounds are written `ss/ß`. PT speakers reverse `ie/ei` because PT *ei* is "ay" (*lei*) — the German *ei* is closer to PT *ai* (*pai*).
> 🇪🇸 **Spanish contrast** — Spanish never capitalises common nouns, days of the week (*el lunes → am **M**ontag*), months (*en mayo → im **M**ai*), or languages (*hablo español → ich spreche **S**panisch*), so check noun capitals last in every text. German has **no `¿ ¡` marks and no acute accents (`á, é, í, ó, ú`)** — only `ä, ö, ü` and `ß`. Pronounce `ei` like Spanish *ai* (*mein*), `ie` as long *i* (*Liebe*), `v` as "f" (*Vater*), `w` as "v" (*Wasser*), and `z` as "ts" (*Zeit*).
> 🇫🇷 **French contrast** — French never capitalises common nouns, so train your eye to capitalise every noun (*das Haus, der Montag*). German `u` sounds like French *ou* (*gut*), while `ü` sounds like French *u* (*Tür*); `ö` sounds like French *eu* (*schön*). German has no acute/grave accents (*é, è, à*) or cedilla (*ç*) — only `ä, ö, ü` and `ß`. In letters, the salutation ends with a comma and the next line starts **lowercase** (unlike French *Madame, / Je vous écris…*).
> 🇬🇧 **English contrast** — English capitalises days, months and languages but not nouns; German capitalises days and months **because they are nouns** and *Deutsch* as a language, but not *deutsch* as an adjective.

## 3. Watch out

- *das* (article/pronoun, "the/that") vs *dass* (conjunction, "that"): *Ich weiß, **dass** **das** Buch gut ist.* Test: if you can replace it with *dieses/welches*, it is *das*.
- *wieder* (again) vs *wider* (against); *Sie* (you-formal / they) vs *sie* (she / they).
- `-ig` at word end sounds like "-ich" but is spelled `ig`: *richtig, wenig, lustig*.
- `v` is pronounced "f" in native words (*Vater, viel, von*) — do not spell them with `f`.
- `st/sp` at word start sound like "scht/schp" but are spelled `st/sp`: *Straße, spielen*.
