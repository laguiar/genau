## 1. The rule in one picture

```
 STEP 1 — choose the stem by the OWNER          STEP 2 — add the ending by the OWNED NOUN (like ein/kein)
   ich  → mein-                                          m       n       f       pl
   du   → dein-                                   ●     mein    mein    meine   meine
   er   → sein-      (his)                        ▲     meinEN  mein    meine   meine
   es   → sein-      (its)
   sie  → ihr-       (her)                        euer + ending drops the e:  eure, euren
   wir  → unser-
   ihr  → euer-  (eur- before an ending)          Sie (formal) → Ihr-  always CAPITAL
   sie  → ihr-       (their)
   Sie  → Ihr-       (your, formal)

   sein Bruder  = his brother  ·  ihr Bruder = her brother  ·  ihre Schwester = her sister  ·  seine Schwester = his sister
```

## 2. The rule in words

**Two decisions.** First, **who owns it?** That gives the stem: *mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr*. Second, **what is owned, and in which case?** That gives the ending, and the endings are exactly those of *ein/kein*: nothing for ● m/n and ▲ n, *-e* for f and plural, *-en* for ▲ m. *Mein Bruder, meine Schwester, mein Kind, meine Eltern. Ich sehe meinen Bruder.*

**`sein` vs `ihr`.** *sein* = the owner is *er* or *es* (his / its); *ihr* = the owner is *sie* (her, or their). The ending still follows the owned noun: *sein**e** Mutter* (his mother — feminine noun, male owner), *ihr Vater* (her father — masculine noun, female owner).

**`ihr` has three meanings**: *her*, *their*, and the pronoun *you (plural)*. Capital *Ihr* = *your* (formal). Verb and context disambiguate: *Ihr Auto ist neu* (your, formal) / *ihr Auto* (her or their) / *Ihr seid nett* (you plural).

**`euer`** loses its second *e* whenever an ending is added: *euer Haus, eur**e** Wohnung, eur**en** Sohn*. *unser* keeps its *e*: *unsere, unseren*.

> 🇧🇷 **Portuguese contrast** — PT *seu/sua* is famously ambiguous (his, her, their, your); *dele/dela* fixes it. German always distinguishes: *dele* = *sein-*, *dela* = *ihr-*, *deles/delas* = *ihr-*. PT agrees the possessive with the owned noun (*seu carro, sua casa*) — same principle as the German ending, so this feels natural; the new part is the case ending (*-en*).
> 🇪🇸 **Spanish contrast** — In Spanish, *su / sus* can mean *his, her, its, their,* or *your (usted/ustedes)* (*su coche*). German separates them by the **owner** first: **sein-** (his/its), **ihr-** (her/their), and capitalised **Ihr-** (your — formal *usted/ustedes*), and then adds the ending for the **possessed noun** (*sein Auto, seine Schwester* vs *ihr Auto, ihre Schwester* vs *Ihr Auto*).
> 🇫🇷 **French contrast** — In French, *son / sa / ses* depends only on the gender of the possessed noun (*son frère, sa sœur* = both "his" and "her"). German works in two steps: first pick the **stem by the owner** (**sein-** = his/its, **ihr-** = her/their), then add the **ending by the noun's gender and case** (*sein Bruder, seine Schwester* vs *ihr Bruder, ihre Schwester*).
> 🇬🇧 **English contrast** — *his/her* choice is identical to *sein/ihr*, but English adds no endings; German does. *its* = *sein-* (neuter owner: *das Kind und sein Spielzeug*).

## 3. Watch out

- ~~ihr Bruder~~ for *his brother* → *sein Bruder*. Owner decides the stem.
- ~~seine Mutter~~ for *her mother* → *ihre Mutter*.
- ▲ masculine: *Ich besuche mein**en** Vater / dein**en** Freund / ihr**en** Bruder.*
- *Ihr* (formal your) capitalised, also in the middle of a sentence: *Wie ist Ihre Adresse?*
- Possessive + noun is one ● slot: *Meine kleine Schwester wohnt hier* (verb second after the whole phrase).
